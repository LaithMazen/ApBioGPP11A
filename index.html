Move More, Live Better: A Biology-Based Response to Physical Inactivity

Driving question: Why is physical inactivity considered a global health issue, and how can biology help us design evidence-based solutions?

The Issue and Why It Matters

Physical inactivity means not doing enough movement to meet health guidelines. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity per day for teenagers. In school, many students sit through lessons, travel by car and spend free time on screens, so reaching this target is hard.

This is a global problem. According to the WHO, about 31% of adults worldwide (around 1.8 billion people) did not do enough physical activity in 2022. The WHO also reports that most adolescents are not active enough. Inactive people have a higher risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers, which puts pressure on health systems everywhere.

Local barrier: At our school, hot weather for much of the year means outdoor breaks are limited, and long lesson blocks keep students seated.

Biological Model: Three Interacting Body Systems

The cardiovascular, respiratory and muscular systems work together as a team.

Muscles use oxygen and energy → the respiratory system takes in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide → the cardiovascular system carries oxygen to muscles and waste away.

SystemMain jobEffect of long-term inactivity
MuscularContracts to produce movement and uses glucose and oxygen for energyMuscles weaken and shrink, and use less glucose, so blood sugar control gets worse
CardiovascularHeart pumps blood carrying oxygen and nutrients around the bodyThe heart becomes less efficient, resting heart rate can rise, and blood vessels become less healthy, raising blood pressure risk
RespiratoryLungs take in oxygen and release carbon dioxideBreathing is less efficient, so people get out of breath more easily during simple tasks

Three consequences of prolonged inactivity:

  1. Weaker heart and higher risk of cardiovascular disease.
  2. Poorer metabolism: muscles take up less glucose, which raises the risk of type 2 diabetes and weight gain.
  3. Lower fitness: weaker muscles and less efficient lungs mean less stamina for daily life.

Graph: Pulse (beats per minute) for each student at rest, right after activity and after 3 minutes of rest.

Interpretation: In every case, pulse rose sharply after activity and then fell during rest. The muscles needed more oxygen, so the heart beat faster to deliver it. This is a negative feedback mechanism: the body detects the change and works to return to its normal level, which is homeostasis. Students with a lower resting pulse (1 and 3) recovered closer to their resting rate, while the student with the highest resting pulse (4) recovered the least. This fits the idea that a fitter heart recovers faster.

Limitation: The sample is very small, and we do not know each person's fitness level, age or how hard they exercised, so we cannot say fitness is the only cause.

Solution: "Active Break" at School

  • Who: All Grade 11 students, led by student volunteers and supported by the Biology teacher.
  • What: A 5-minute active break in the middle of long lessons, with simple stretching, walking and body-weight movements.
  • Where: In the classroom or corridor, so the heat does not matter.
  • How often: Once per long lesson, and at least once every school day.
  • Materials: None needed, only a printed card of exercises and a timer.
  • Inclusion and safety: Every movement has an easy option, students can sit and do arm and shoulder movements, and no one has to share health information or push beyond what is comfortable.

Why It Works, and How We Will Measure Success

Short movement breaks make muscles use oxygen and glucose, make the heart and lungs work together, and break up long periods of sitting. Doing this regularly supports heart health, blood sugar control and muscle strength, and the WHO notes that being active also improves mental well-being and concentration.

Two success indicators:

  1. Participation rate: At least 80% of students join in the active break each week.
  2. Break frequency: Active breaks happen in at least 4 out of 5 school days each week.

Reflection and References

Reflection: Before this project I thought exercise was only about looking fit. Now I understand that the heart, lungs and muscles depend on each other, and that inactivity damages all three. The pulse data showed me how the body regulates itself to keep balance. The hardest part was explaining the data clearly, but making a simple graph helped. I learned that small, realistic changes at school can make a real difference to health.
